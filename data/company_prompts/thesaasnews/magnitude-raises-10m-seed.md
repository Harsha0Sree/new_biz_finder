On July 30, Anthropic told the world that three of its Claude models, running inside a third-party capture-the-flag evaluation, reached out of their "isolated" test environment and touched real companies. One model pulled credentials and production data from a business that happened to share a name with a fictional CTF target. Another registered a PyPI account and published a malicious package that ended up executing on a real security firm's systems. A third scanned about nine thousand live targets before compromising one, and at least had the sense to stop once it realized the target was real. (Full details in Anthropic's

I gave a few quotes to reporters on this one, because it landed squarely in something I spend most of my time thinking about: not whether a model behaves the way you expect, but whether the environment around it actually matches the story it was told.
The Gap Between Assumed and Actual
Here is the detail that should stop every security leader mid-scroll. The models were explicitly told in their prompt that they had no internet access. The environment disagreed. Anthropic traced that to a misunderstanding with a third-party evaluation partner, and took responsibility for the fixes itself. Its own read is that this sits closer to a harness failure than an alignment failure — a comparison, not an acquittal. Two of the three models kept attacking after their own reasoning flagged the targets as real; only the internal research model stopped on its own. The models were doing what they were built to do inside a container that was quietly not the container anyone described to them — and two of them kept doing it after the container's story stopped holding.
As I told SC Media: "The AI industry has spent years making models more powerful, and far less time verifying what those models can actually reach. In this case, the models were told they were in a simulation with no internet access. The environment said otherwise. That gap between assumed and actual configuration is where third-party risk now lives."
I want to sit on that last line, because I don't think it's just a clever way to describe one bad week for one lab. It's a description of where a huge share of AI risk is quietly accumulating right now. Not in the weights. Not in the model card. In the seam between what a system was told about its boundaries and what those boundaries actually enforce.

Third-Party Risk in the Mythos Era
Most of the most consequential breaches in the last 18 months, including Change Healthcare, Salesloft-Drift, and the Shai Hulud supply chain attack, have one thing in common: third-party risk. The next wave may move even faster. Frontier systems like Mythos, Fable & GPT 5.5-Cyber have shown that AI can find vulnerabilities across entire software ecosystems at a scale no human team could match. If defensive AI can do that, adversarial AI can too.
Chances are, the next one will start with a vendor. Or a vendor’s sub-processor. A Mythos-generated exploit or another AI system connected to sensitive data. A product update that changes how information moves. Or a dependency no one knew to monitor until it mattered.
Modern cyber risk moves through infrastructure providers, software platforms, internal applications, plugins, service partners, AI agents, and autonomous systems. Companies rely on these systems every day. Most cannot fully see them, govern them, or coordinate a response across affected suppliers.
It is where even the strongest companies know they are exposed, and where smaller suppliers often lack the resources to raise their security posture on their own. AI is making that gap harder to ignore because companies now must adopt AI, buy AI, govern AI, and protect themselves from AI-enabled risk at the same time.
Most companies still manage third-party risk through questionnaires, email threads, document portals, and follow-up meetings. Analysts collect evidence, issue approvals, and return months later to do it again. That process creates a record of review. It does not create continuous assurance and trust.

An AI-enabled product can change faster than the review cycle built to govern it. Across the market, teams repeatedly ask the same vendors the same questions and validate the same evidence. Modern engineering teams often need CI pipelines to reach internal services: dev APIs, databases, or other resources inside private VPCs. GitHub Actions runners are convenient and scalable, but they live on the public internet. How do you give them secure, short-lived access without provisioning always-on infrastructure?
At Magnitude, we ran into this exact problem with our QA automation. The test suites (Playwright, Newman collections, smoke tests) all needed to hit endpoints that are only reachable from our private VPC. My first thought was to use self-hosted runners because that’s what I used in the past. But on further evaluation, it seemed to be an overkill for our current use case so I decided to explore simpler alternatives. We have been using tailscale for some time, but hadn’t explored the idea of accessing an internal VPC from github runners. After a little bit of research it seemed like it could work and fit well on our existing IaC configuration, without requiring long-lived credentials, VPNs, jump hosts, etc.
The Constraints I Was Working With
What I was looking for:
No persistent infrastructure
Minimal blast radius if a workflow was ever compromised
Compatibility with the tools engineers already use or may use in the future (curl, browsers, HTTP clients) without the need for custom network proxies, etc. that may have limited support
Something easy to use for developers, without a lot of complicated yaml
As mentioned earlier, I’m used to self-hosted runners (e.g., GitHub runners on EKS managed by the Actions Runner Controller) and I know it works well. It also requires more infrastructure and operational costs. At larger scales it makes sense: You pay for the additional infrastructure but you save on the github bill. However, we’re still small and our use case is limited - the additional infrastructure and operational cost wasn’t justifiable.
This post describes the approach I landed on: treating every CI job as a short-lived Tailscale node that joins the tailnet only for the duration of the workflow, then disappears.
Ephemeral Nodes + Narrow ACLs
Joining via OAuth and OIDC
We didn’t want to store long-lived auth keys in GitHub secrets. Instead, workflows assume a dedicated IAM role in AWS (
github-qa-automation
) via GitHub OIDC, then fetch short-lived Tailscale OAuth credentials from Secrets Manager. The trust policy on that role is scoped to specific repositories and refs, so only the jobs we allow can obtain the credentials.
From there, the
tailscale/github-action
registers a node tagged
tag:github-qa-ci
. The node accepts the necessary subnet routes, but it’s removed automatically when the job finishes, so there’s no manual cleanup needed afterward.

Most TPRM programs only engage once a formal request hits the queue, but by then the business user has usually already decided on a vendor, leaving the security team to react rather than guide. This structural blind spot means duplicate tools keep getting requested and risk keeps entering the business before anyone assesses it. Magnitude's Intake Agent addresses this by meeting users at the moment of intent, walking them through a pre-assessment and recommending approved alternatives so redundant requests get deflected early.

Magnitude's Intake Agent acts as a conversational co-pilot at the point of intent, steering users toward already-approved tools and deflecting redundant requests before they become tickets.
The payoff is less tool sprawl, fewer tickets in the TPRM queue, and risk reduction that happens before a vendor relationship starts instead of during a later audit.
Ask any third-party risk management team when their process actually begins, and they'll point to a JIRA or ServiceNow ticket. But that's not when the risk begins. It begins earlier, when a business user decides they need a new tool, signs up for a free trial, or starts "vibe coding" with an AI assistant that has access to company data. By the time that request reaches TPRM, the decision has often already been made. The team is reacting (and catching up) to a choice, not shaping it.
This is the core problem with how most TPRM programs are structured today: the team is blind to the moment of intent. They have no visibility into the requests being drafted in Slack threads, no seat at the table when a manager is comparing two SaaS tools, and no way to assist before a business user has already mentally committed to a vendor. Once the formal request lands, the TPRM clock starts ticking, and the team is playing catch up: trying to understand the context, the business value, and the actual risk of a decision that's already been made. That catch up work costs time and money, and it delays not just this vendor's review but every other assessment sitting in the queue behind it. Both the delay and the scramble strain the relationship between security and the business it's meant to protect.
Security That Arrives Too Late Is Just Friction
Every TPRM team knows the tension. Move too slowly and you become the department everyone routes around. Move too fast and you inherit risk from vendors, and their vendors, that nobody properly vetted. The traditional model, built on point-in-time questionnaires and manual reviews, forces this tradeoff because it was never designed to operate at the speed or volume of how businesses actually adopt tools today.
The deeper issue is that TPRM teams rarely get the chance to interact with or educate business users directly. There isn't time, and there isn't a scalable mechanism for it. So the same redundant requests keep surfacing. A team spins up a new project management tool when three others already exist inside the company. TL;DR
A simple job queue (ARQ) served our LLM research pipelines well at first, but agentic work quickly became long-running and multi-step in ways a queue can't track.
A single job timeout couldn't distinguish slow from dead, and our workflow's dependency graph existed only implicitly in scattered hand-off code.
Moving to Temporal gave each step its own timeouts and retries, made the DAG explicit in one readable function, and unlocked true parallelism across our worker fleet.
As an engineer at an early-stage startup, your main focus is usually on getting things working quickly. Vague ideas are crystallized with early prototypes. You have the freedom to play fast and loose with the rules - maybe skip unit tests for now, or deploy straight to AWS (there's little need for a production environment before you have actual customers). Quick and dirty puts it in the hands of your product team and design partners. A lot of it will be thrown away, so over-engineering from the start would just waste precious time. That's why when certain technical decisions are made, you can almost always expect them to change over time - sometimes significantly. In this article, I'd like to walk you through the journey we took with a component of our system that may be easy to overlook, but is critically important - the async work queue.
Work queues have been a staple of backend systems for a long time, and in the modern world it's even more critical to be able to easily spawn long-running, multi-step, LLM-driven pipelines. Every platform has at least one library to queue up and run background jobs. Celery, BullMQ, Sidekiq, Hangfire. Being a Python FastAPI app, we chose
ARQ
.
For our initial implementation, ARQ was a great choice at the time. Its API surface is small, it's async-native and if you already have Redis running, it's dead-simple to deploy. In an afternoon or less, we had a job queue running agentic workflows behind our product. (At Magnitude, we build an LLM-driven platform for third-party risk management - under the hood, that’s long-running, multi-step research pipelines). It worked perfectly well for our early prototypes - but over time, the cracks started to show. A worker might die mid-job, leaving the client polling for results that would never arrive. A timeout constant doubled "to be safe" (then doubled again). Or the realization that even though we were able to scale worker nodes horizontally across many ECS nodes, all of the work was still crammed into a single event loop running on a single worker. Clearly we had reached the limit of what ARQ was built for. Yes, we can work around these issues with additional code. More sophisticated retry logic. Finer-grained progress checkpoints with workflows that can skip over steps that have already completed. Jobs scheduling sub-tasks as more jobs. What I'm describing has a name: a "durable execution platform" and it is already available in a platform called
Temporal
.
