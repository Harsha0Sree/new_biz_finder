Source Title: Pramaana Labs raises $27M seed round from Khosla Ventures to bring formal verification to AI | TechCrunch

As enterprises struggle to turn AI pilot programs into functional parts of their business, reliability has taken center stage. A new startup is hoping to solve that problem by drawing on the tools of mathematical formalization, combining one of computer science’s most reliable systems with one of its most chaotic.
On Wednesday,
Pramaana Labs
announced $27 million in seed funding led by Khosla Ventures, with participation from Accel, BoldCap, Nexus Venture Partners, Premji Invest, and Unbound.
Pramaana will focus on highly sensitive verticals like law, drug discovery, and tax preparation — where errors can be costly and reliability is at a premium. Deploying AI in those systems will require stronger protections against hallucinations and errors than we currently have. But as Pramaana co-founder and CEO Ranjan Rajagopalan sees it, they’re also uniquely suited to formalization.
“It’s like math in the sense that you have a lot of rules that you need to abide by,” Rajagopalan told TechCrunch, describing the rules of the tax code. “Once you have a codified version of it, the reasoning on top of it starts becoming deterministic.”
Pramaana’s system still runs on a conventional LLM, giving it the flexibility to answer natural language questions and tackle complex problems that conventional computers can’t handle. But there’s a deterministic layer on top of that LLM ensuring the LLM’s work checks out.
This combination of an LLM engine with deterministic verification is
a popular setup
; Pramaana’s unique approach is to use the tools of formal verification — drawing on the open source LEAN programming language used to verify mathematical proofs. There’s real precedent for much of this work; Rajagopalan points to
France’s CATALA project
, which formalizes much of the country’s tax and benefit system into executable code.
For each use case, Pramaana will build its own LEAN-style formal verification system, overseen by domain experts. For tax law, the company is working with former IRS commissioner Danny Werfel, while professors from IIT Delhi, IIT Madras, and UC Berkeley oversee the cybersecurity and drug discovery system.
“The world’s hardest problems are not unsolvable. They are unformalized,” says Rajagopalan. “Every domain where being wrong can cost someone their health, money, or freedom has rules.”
Now, those rules just need to be codified.

How Lean Handles Ambiguity, Vagueness, and Gaps in Law
Three Formalization Blockers and How to Deal with Them.
Author
Author
Yoshiki
Yoshiki
Share
TL;DR
Laws written in natural language often contain ambiguity, vagueness, and gaps. The assumption is that the interpreter will interpret flexibly and reasonably when encountering new situations. Formalizations in Lean can contain none of these. Ambiguity, vagueness, and gaps are not proof that law resists formalization. They are distinct problems that can be surfaced, tested, refined, and handled systematically. We take this blog to show how Pramaana tackles all three formalization challenges.
Law Formalization is a Tractable Problem
Since laws prescribe future behavior, legal drafters face the challenge of writing for an uncertain future. Words may gain new meaning, leading to genuine ambiguities, thresholds of vague definitions might shift, and new situations might arise that fall through gaps in the text. This does not mean that law dissolves into pure guesswork. We should instead turn our attention to interpretive practices developed over centuries that stabilize outcomes and even leverage these linguistic uncertainties.
By leveraging Lean4, Pramaana’s system already satisfies the self-consistency criteria. Self-consistency is a key property that is used to ensure that, while different legal actors might have different interpretations, they are not giving contradictory answers that would result in a provable falsehood. Ensuring this property by default means that a large class of legal hallucinations are removed by construction.
Beyond self-consistency, our domain formalization system handles ambiguity, vagueness, and gaps through a series of proven approaches. It removes ambiguities by automatically generating examples that distinguish multiple interpretations. The domain formalizer generates formal representations that are provably complete but get incrementally detailed. And the formalizer automatically detects gaps in the natural language specification by leveraging the lean type system.
Example: Robbery at a Bank
Consider the following (fictional) statute:
All robberies committed at a bank shall be punished with additional five years of imprisonment.
Now let’s see how ambiguity, vagueness, and gaps can arise even in this short clause.
Ambiguity
: Does “bank” here refer to a financial institution or the river bank?
Vagueness
: suppose the interpretation is “financial institution.” How close do I have to be a branch of a bank for this to apply? What happens to robbers who wait at the door of a bank? What about the sidewalk or the other side of the street?
Gap
: suppose that the majority of the population now use mobile applications for banking. Do robberies of people looking at their phone bank apps count?
What Lean Can Already Do: Consistency
While different judges interpret the same law slightly differently, we would rightly be suspicious if a judge interpreted “bank” as a financial institution on even days and river embankments on odd days. In the absence of perfect ground truth, self-consistency goes a long way. By formalizing the law in lean, we avoid inconsistencies by construction. When a lean code typechecks, inconsistencies are no longer possible because the entire system is checked to be consistent through the use of a type system. The river bank and the financial institution would have different types, causing a type error if the two interpretations were mixed. By construction, Pramaana’s AI will not emit mixed interpretations.
Typechecking in lean prevents many real-world tax audits that start with inconsistent answers. Consider the case of a man who moved from
New York to Florida for tax purposes
. A tax audit was triggered when he accidentally applied for a flyfishing license as a New York resident. Because its answers are checked by the lean type checker, Pramaana’s AI will never give such inconsistent answers. While it does choose one interpretation, it won’t mix incompatible interpretations that yield contradictions and trigger audits.

From lofty conconstitutional clauses to granular instructions on how to fill a tax form, legal constraints operate in nested refinements that get more and more precise as the abstraction level goes down. Pramaana Solves IMO 2026 in Lean 4 in Under 7 Hours
Pramaana’s Panini and Hardy systems formalized and proved all six IMO 2026 problems in Lean 4 in under seven hours and for less than $150.
Author
Author

Pramaana's formalization pipeline translated all 6 problem statements of IMO 2026 into formally verified Lean 4 proofs in under 7 hours, at a compute cost of < $150.
Pramaana builds formally verified systems to check claims and decisions made in the real world. In these settings, success isn't just about eventually finding a solution and verifying it, it's about doing so within real-world time and budget constraints.
Verification involves producing both an answer and a chain of reasoning that shows why the answer is valid. The structure of that reasoning varies from domain to domain. Some domains require deep reasoning, following long sequences of deductions with relatively little branching. Others reward breadth instead, navigating thousands of interacting rules, each simple to check, where the prover’s job is to move through them quickly (for more on how we think about the spectrum of provers, see The Age of Everyday Provers).
The domains we focus on at Pramaana fall into the latter category, where breadth and speed matter most – for example, tax, law, and drug discovery. Competition mathematics sits in the former, where solving problems requires long chains of creative reductions, where depth and ingenuity matters. The question we ask is can a prover optimized for breadth and speed adapt to a domain that rewards depth and creativity?
The International Mathematical Olympiad, held on 15th–16th July, 2026 in Shanghai, provided an ideal benchmark.
Panini, our autoformalizer, translated each problem into a formal Lean 4 statement, which Hardy, our prover, then solved and proved.

Panini: Faithfully formalizing the question
How a problem is formalized matters as much as how it is proved. A formalizer must accurately translate the original question into Lean 4. In practice, however, it is common for a model to silently strengthen the problem by deducing part of the solution or introducing subtle assumptions that were never stated. If that happens, the prover is no longer solving the original problem, but an easier one. Unless those assumptions are themselves proved to follow from the original statement, the solution is incomplete. We discuss a concrete example of this in the section "A hidden assumption in Question 3" below.
Panini produced a formalization of every problem in a single attempt. Before proving began, each formalization was reviewed by a human.
The result
Neither Panini nor Hardy had access to the internet, and the knowledge cutoff of both models predates the competition, the problems were entirely unseen. The pipeline produced formally verified solutions to all six problems.

Hardy, Pramaana's prover, outperforms Leanstral 1.5 on statutory reasoning and software verification showing that math-specific proof strategies don't transfer efficiently to other domains.

A specialized prover can beat a much larger general model on the domain it was built for. On PutnamBench, the math-specific Leanstral 1.5 delivers performance comparable to frontier models at less than one-tenth of the price. But that specialization does not carry over: on
SARA
(statutory reasoning) and
VeriSoftBench
(software verification), Leanstral needs roughly an order of magnitude more budget to reach the scores reached by Hardy. Hardy is Pramaana's proving system: our harness paired with our post-trained prover model. At Pramaana, we are building in-house formal prover models not only for mathematics, but also for real-world domains such as tax, law, and software verification.
Key Takeaways
This work represents Pramaana’s first step toward a broader family of in-house foundation prover models.
Leanstral 1.5 delivers impressive results on PutnamBench, but does not generalize efficiently to other domains, including tax and software verification. This limitation is particularly visible under shorter, more realistic inference budgets. Leanstral's headline PutnamBench result, for example, is reported at pass@4M tokens.
These results echo the argument made in our earlier post,
The Age of Everyday Provers
: different verification domains reward different capabilities, and no single search strategy is likely to dominate every setting.
The combination of Pramaana’s harness and post-trained in-house prover model performs strongly across multiple domains, particularly at lower token budgets. This more closely matches the practical constraints of Pramaana’s real-world use cases.
Evaluation Setup
Datasets
We evaluated the models on two datasets:
SARA: Statutory reasoning, with 112 problems
VeriSoftBench: Software verification, with 163 problems
Quality Control
SARA was released by Holzenberger et al. as natural-language statutory reasoning tasks with an accompanying Prolog encoding. SARA Clean-112 is our Lean 4 formalization of the tasks.
Before evaluation, every problem was checked for:
Successful compilation in Lean 4.27
Use of native_decide
Use of sorry or admit in dependencies
Reliance on nonstandard axioms
Answer leakage in dependencies
Additionally, VeriSoftBench was stratified to include a maximum of 10 problems from each repository. When more than 10 problems remained in a repository after quality control, the final problems were selected randomly.
Models and Harnesses
The following model and harness combinations were evaluated:
Qwen 3.6-27B, using the Pramaana harness
Pramaana post-trained Qwen 3.6-27B, using the Pramaana harness

Announcement
AI can produce the answer. It cannot yet prove the answer is right.
In critical domains like tax, law, medicine, finance, and governance, that distinction decides everything. This distinction creates an accountability and trust gap. To be honest, a HOLE.
We started Pramaana to close this gap.
The idea is simple: AI should not just sound like an expert. It should be able to prove its work.
Jim Simons spent his career turning one messy human domain, the markets, into math. Renaissance Technologies didn't trade on hunches. It traded on math, and it became the most profitable fund in history. The lesson was clear: when math enters a particular domain, it solves it.
A couple of years ago, AlphaProof showed the next step. Given some of the hardest problems in competition mathematics, a machine did not just find the answers. It wrote the formal, machine-checked proofs for them, in Lean. The proving itself was automated.
Lean proved theorems. We're building the infrastructure that lets it prove the real world.
That is the announcement. Here is the conviction behind it.
The First Wave: Fluency Over Truth
Every technology that reshaped civilization arrived in two waves. First creation, the messy, generative, world-making wave. Then proof is the slower wave that makes the new thing trustworthy enough for everyone to actually depend on it.
The first wave of AI was fluency and creation, and Transformers were the breakthrough. They gave us models that write, draw, code, and converse, and they rewarded one thing above all: speed. So the industry poured its capital into compute, the infrastructure to scale fluency. It worked. We got astonishing productivity and a real sense that the future had arrived early.
But fluency is not the same as truth.
While the world debates whether AI will take every job or launch every weapon, we still live in a world where ambulances sit in traffic, chronic disease goes undiagnosed, and an ordinary person cannot understand their own taxes in 2026. The most dangerous thing an AI can produce isn't a wrong answer. It's a confidently wrong answer that no one is accountable for.

Multiply that one signature across every doctor, lawyer, and accountant alive, and you have the real ceiling on what AI can do for the world.
